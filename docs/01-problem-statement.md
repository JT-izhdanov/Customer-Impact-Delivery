# 01 — Problem Statement: Why the Merger Didn't Produce Collaboration

*Revised after Advisory Board session 01 (see `docs/advisory-board/01-session-problem-statement.md`).*

## What we did

Last year we consolidated into four practices — Data & AI, Copilot & Dynamics, Security & Compliance (incl. Azure), and Business Central ERP — with the explicit goal of driving more cross-practice collaboration.

## What actually happened

We still sell and deliver predominantly practice-specific technology. Cross-practice work is opportunistic, not systematic.

## Why this matters now — regardless of the merger

Even if the merger had worked, the model it optimized is aging out:

- **The effort-based project model is being compressed by AI.** TSIA calls PS 1.0 — billable effort, one-time projects — "breaking"; SPI's 2025 benchmark shows billable utilization at its all-time low (66.4%) even as margins hold; McKinsey cites AI-compressed delivery as the reason ~25% of its fees are now outcome-based (research 03).
- **Microsoft now pays for adoption and usage, not deals sold.** MAU-based accelerators, usage-weighted partner scores, MCEM stage-5 value realization — the ecosystem's cash flow has moved to the post-sale stages (research 01).
- Four practices collaborating beautifully on effort-based, one-time projects would still be the wrong revenue model. The 2027 plan must fix both problems at once.

## Root-cause diagnosis

The merger changed reporting lines. It did not change any of the mechanisms that determine what gets sold and delivered:

### 0. Leadership's part
- Leadership commissioned a structural fix while keeping single-practice quotas, utilization targets, and P&Ls in place — announcing "collaborate" while the paycheck said "don't."
- The merger was designed as lines-and-boxes; practice leads and sellers were not contracted with about what they wanted or feared from it.
- **We own this.** A diagnosis that locates all causes in sellers, systems, and Microsoft — and none in the people asking others to change — repeats the mistake.

### 1. Leads arrive tech-labeled, and we accept the label
- Customers call saying "we need Power BI help." Microsoft referrals arrive tagged to a workload. Our intake routes the label straight to the matching practice.
- **Result:** the outcome conversation never happens — and worse, accepting a customer's self-diagnosis makes us a *responsive supplier*: comparable line-by-line against every partner running the same workload, and therefore price-shopped. The mid-market is full of near-identical $5K assessments (research 04). Foregone expansion is the smaller cost; commodity margin erosion is the larger one.

### 2. Sellers *won't* challenge the label (incentives)
- Bringing in a second practice adds coordination cost, scoping risk, and a longer cycle — with no personal upside under quota pressure.
- **Result:** rational sellers sell single-practice deals.

### 3. Sellers *can't* challenge the label (capability)
- Challenging a customer's self-diagnosis requires *teaching them something they don't know about their own business* — a prepared, data-backed reframe that leads back to us. That insight content (per-play benchmarks, cost-of-status-quo data, teaching narratives) largely doesn't exist today (assumption — validate via insight inventory, below).
- Fix comp and intake tomorrow, and a willing seller still has nothing to open with beyond generic questions. Good questions without insight are just slower solution selling.
- **Result:** even motivated sellers default to what they can confidently scope.

### 4. Delivery staffs from its own bench
- Utilization targets live at the practice level; practice leads protecting utilization staff from their own bench first. No standing mechanism makes cross-practice staffing the default.
- **Result:** even expandable deals get delivered narrow.

### 5. Compensation and P&L reward silos
- Quotas, practice P&Ls, and utilization metrics are all single-practice. Multi-practice deals create attribution friction — a tax on collaboration; sellers avoid taxes.
- **Result:** the org chart says "collaborate," the paycheck says "don't."

### 6. Microsoft's motions reinforce the silos
- Designations, specializations, and funding programs are workload-aligned; our marketing and certifications organize around workloads accordingly.
- **Result:** ecosystem gravity pulls toward tech-stack selling; we've done nothing to counteract it.

### 7. Nobody owns the customer's outcome — and our revenue model ends at go-live
- No standard artifact states the business result we signed up to influence; no post-go-live cadence measures it. The customer is failed first: nobody is accountable for whether the promised result happened.
- The commercial consequence compounds it: we monetize only the "Land" stage while the ecosystem's economics (and Microsoft's incentives) moved to Adopt/Expand/Renew. Our renewable-revenue share is unmeasured (assumption — validate below).
- **Result:** customers can't see their impact, and we have neither the evidence base nor the recurring revenue that post-sale ownership creates. Outcome ownership must hold even when we advise a customer *not* to buy — that is what keeps self-interest out of the motion.

### 8. The merger created no trust between practices
- Research on merged professional-services firms is specific: they lack **competence trust** — "people just don't know how good the other players are" (Gardner, research 02). A seller won't introduce a colleague they don't trust to *their* client, no matter how the crediting works.
- Compounding it: client relationships are owned by individuals, not the firm. Cross-practice expansion asks individuals to share access they experience as personal risk.
- **Result:** fix every incentive above and collaboration can still stall on trust. Falsification test: if cleanly credited cross-practice deals still don't form in the pilot, the binding constraint is trust, not incentives.

### The emotional layer (runs through all of the above)
Resistance to the 2027 shift will be emotional, not just economic: fear of scoping the unfamiliar, fear of losing control of "my" client or "my" bench, fear of looking incompetent in front of a sister practice. More structure and better data do not answer feelings; involvement and acknowledgment do. The rollout must be designed for this (see doc 05 enablement, doc 07 governance).

## The crux (proposed — for leadership ratification)

Nine causes invite nine workstreams; a strategy needs a pivot point. **Proposed crux: causes 1 + 5 — the deal is shaped and credited before any second practice can enter.** Everything downstream (staffing, delivery, outcome ownership) inherits the shape of the deal at intake. Concentrating the pilot on (a) outcome-first deal shaping with real insight content and (b) friction-free attribution should unlock the rest; causes 3 and 8 are the enabling investments (insight content, engineered familiarity); causes 0, 4, 6, 7 are addressed in sequence, not simultaneously. **Open decision for Igor:** ratify or overrule this bet — and name what consciously stays unfixed through 2027.

## The lesson

**Structure doesn't create collaboration; incentives, motions, artifacts — and trust — do.** A second reorg would repeat the mistake. The table below is a *guiding-policy preview* (the full policy lives in docs 02–08), not a settled remedy; sequencing and pilot scope are open decisions:

| Lever | Today | 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| What we sell | Technology projects | Business outcomes (Customer Impact) |
| How deals start | Tech-labeled lead → matching practice | Outcome discovery → cross-practice solution |
| How we scope | Practice SOW | Impact Hypothesis + phased SOW |
| How we staff | Practice bench | Outcome-scoped pods |
| How we measure | Delivered on time/budget | Realized customer impact |
| How we pay | Single-practice credit | Written attribution + multi-practice incentive |
| How practices know each other | They don't (competence-trust gap) | Engineered familiarity: joint deal reviews, co-staffing, internal proof roadshows |
| What we invest ahead of revenue | Nothing budgeted | Explicit transition line item ("the fish"): outcome-stewardship time (PM/PL/account), measurement infrastructure, comp accelerators |

**Deliberately unchanged in 2027** (declared constants — doc 11): the **Proven Process** (all five stages, gates, and metrics), the **three project roles** (PM / PL / Project Developers, per the PM/PL operating standard), the four-practice structure, practice-level delivery methodologies, and small single-stack work below the deal threshold. A visible not-changing list is part of the strategy.

## What this diagnosis forces us to choose (inputs to docs 04–05, 09)

The lesson table hides where-to-play choices that must be made explicitly, not defaulted:

- **Which leads** get the outcome-discovery motion vs. which tech-labeled leads we happily fulfill as-is (deal-size floor, account criteria).
- **Which accounts** — those with multi-practice potential and a reachable metric-owning executive — and which industries (2–3 per play) where we can credibly benchmark impact.
- **Which practices' plays launch first** — pilot scope is exclusion, not emphasis.

## Assumptions to validate (Q4 2026)

- Current % of bookings involving 2+ practices (suspected <10% — needs measurement).
- **Current % of revenue that is renewable** (bills without a new SOW) — and who owns that number.
- Current lead sources and how they're labeled at intake.
- Which accounts already buy from 2+ practices (pilot candidates).
- **Trusted-advisor map:** which individuals hold executive-level trusted relationships, per top account — these people and accounts define the pilot.
- **Insight inventory:** what teachable, data-backed insights (benchmarks, cost-of-status-quo data) exist per practice today; which sellers already exhibit challenger-style behavior (pilot staffing input).
- **Validate the diagnosis with the diagnosed:** put this document in front of sellers and practice leads, ask what's missing and what they fear about the shift, and record their additions visibly here before finalizing.
