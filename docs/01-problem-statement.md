# 01 — Problem Statement: Why the Merger Didn't Produce Collaboration

## What we did

Last year we consolidated into four practices — Data & AI, Copilot & Dynamics, Security & Compliance (incl. Azure), and Business Central ERP — with the explicit goal of driving more cross-practice collaboration.

## What actually happened

We still sell and deliver predominantly practice-specific technology. Cross-practice work is opportunistic, not systematic.

## Root-cause diagnosis

The merger changed reporting lines. It did not change any of the mechanisms that actually determine what gets sold and delivered:

### 1. Leads arrive tech-labeled, and we accept the label
- Customers call saying "we need Power BI help" or "we're moving to Business Central." Microsoft referrals arrive tagged to a workload.
- Our intake routes the tech label straight to the matching practice. Nobody is tasked with asking "what business result is behind this request?"
- **Result:** the outcome conversation never happens; the tech scope becomes the deal.

### 2. Sellers scope what they know
- Sellers (and pre-sales architects) are deepest in one stack. Under quota pressure, they propose what they can confidently scope and close.
- Bringing in a second practice adds coordination cost, scoping risk, and a longer sales cycle — with no personal upside.
- **Result:** rational sellers sell single-practice deals.

### 3. Delivery staffs from its own bench
- Utilization targets live at the practice level. A practice lead protecting utilization staffs from their own bench first.
- There is no standing mechanism (pod, deal desk, capacity view) that makes cross-practice staffing the default.
- **Result:** even deals that could expand across practices get delivered narrow.

### 4. Compensation and P&L reward silos
- Quotas, practice P&Ls, and utilization metrics are all single-practice. Multi-practice deals create attribution friction — who gets the credit, whose margin, whose bench?
- Friction on splits is a tax on collaboration; sellers avoid taxes.
- **Result:** the org chart says "collaborate," the paycheck says "don't."

### 5. Microsoft's motions reinforce the silos
- Solution Partner designations, specializations, and funding programs (e.g., pre-sales/deployment incentives) are workload-aligned.
- Our marketing, certifications, and partner scorecards naturally organize around workloads, which flows into how we present ourselves.
- **Result:** the ecosystem gravity is toward tech-stack selling; we've done nothing to counteract it.

### 6. Nobody owns the customer's outcome
- Engagements end at go-live. There is no standard artifact that states the business result we signed up to influence, and no post-go-live cadence that measures it.
- Without a measured outcome, there's no evidence to sell the next practice's work — the strongest natural cross-sell trigger doesn't exist.
- **Result:** we leave expansion revenue and customer credibility on the table.

## The lesson

**Structure doesn't create collaboration; incentives, motions, and artifacts do.** A second reorg would repeat the mistake. The 2027 plan therefore leaves the four-practice structure alone and changes:

| Lever | Today | 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| What we sell | Technology projects | Business outcomes (Customer Impact) |
| How deals start | Tech-labeled lead → matching practice | Outcome discovery → cross-practice solution |
| How we scope | Practice SOW | Impact Hypothesis + phased SOW |
| How we staff | Practice bench | Outcome-scoped pods |
| How we measure | Delivered on time/budget | Realized customer impact |
| How we pay | Single-practice credit | Written attribution + multi-practice incentive |

## Assumptions to validate (Q4 2026)

- Current % of bookings involving 2+ practices (suspected <10% — needs measurement).
- Current lead sources and how they're labeled at intake.
- Whether any existing accounts already buy from 3+ practices (these are pilot candidates).
