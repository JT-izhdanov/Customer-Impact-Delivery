# The Customer Impact Virtual Advisory Board

A panel of ten voices (via their published work) we consult as we design the 2027 Customer Impact plan. Each seat lists: the core ideas from their book(s), what they contribute to this specific question, and the questions they would ask of our plan. Use the questions as a standing review checklist — every major doc in this repo should survive a "board session."

**The question before the board:** How does a four-practice Microsoft partner shift from selling practice-specific technology to selling measurable business outcomes — and make it stick where a structural merger didn't?

---

## Seat 1 — David H. Maister, Charles H. Green, Robert M. Galford
### *The Trusted Advisor* — The Relationship Seat

**Core ideas:**
- The **Trust Equation**: Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) / **Self-Orientation**. The denominator dominates — nothing destroys trust faster than the client sensing you're there to sell.
- The advisor ladder: service offering → needs-based → relationship-based → **trusted advisor** (consulted on issues far beyond your specialty).
- Trust is built in the *engagement process itself* — listening, framing, envisioning, committing — not in credentials.
- (Maister's wider work, *Managing the Professional Service Firm* / "The One-Firm Firm," adds: practice-level profit centers kill collaboration; firm-first cultures win.)

**Contribution to Customer Impact:**
- Outcome selling **is** low self-orientation made operational: leading with the customer's metric instead of our tech stack is the Trust Equation's denominator at work.
- The right to an executive outcome conversation is *earned through the ladder* — a partner known only as "the Power BI vendor" can't skip to trusted advisor by changing its pitch deck. The plays and paid discoveries are rungs, not shortcuts.
- Warns that cross-sell pressure (comp accelerators, quotas) raises perceived self-orientation and can *destroy* the very relationships expansion depends on.

**They would ask us:**
1. Does any part of your sales motion make the customer feel sold *to* rather than advised? Where does your comp design leak self-orientation into the conversation?
2. Which individuals at JourneyTeam are already trusted advisors to a customer executive — and is your pilot built around *them*?
3. Are you willing to advise a customer *not* to buy something from you? What in the plan proves it?

## Seat 2 — Peter Block
### *Flawless Consulting* — The Contracting & Authenticity Seat

**Core ideas:**
- Consulting fails at the **contracting phase**, not delivery: unclear wants, unnamed concerns, and lopsided responsibility doom projects before they start.
- **50/50 responsibility**: the client owns the problem and the change; the consultant who takes 100% becomes a "pair of hands" and the outcome dies at handoff.
- **Authenticity**: say what you see, name resistance directly. Resistance is emotional, not rational — meet it with acknowledgment, not more data.
- Three consultant roles: expert, pair-of-hands, **collaborator** — only the collaborator role produces lasting change.

**Contribution to Customer Impact:**
- The Impact Hypothesis is a *contracting* artifact: it makes wants explicit, names the customer's 50% (data access, exec sponsorship, adoption effort), and surfaces whether the client actually wants the outcome or just the software.
- If the customer won't co-own the baseline and the change management, we are a pair of hands and should price ourselves as one — Block gives us the language to walk away or reframe.
- Equally important **internally**: our sellers' and practice leads' resistance to this plan is emotional (loss of control, fear of scoping the unfamiliar). Announcing frameworks won't move them; naming the resistance and involving them in the design will.

**They would ask us:**
1. In each play, what exactly is the *customer's* 50% — and what happens contractually when they don't deliver it?
2. Where in your rollout are you treating your own sellers' resistance as an information problem when it's really a loss-of-control problem?
3. Is your discovery authentic inquiry, or a choreographed path to a predetermined proposal?

## Seat 3 — Mahan Khalsa & Randy Illig
### *Let's Get Real or Let's Not Play* — The Sales Conversation Seat

**Core ideas:**
- **"Helping clients succeed"** is the sales method: the intent to help, made rigorous. Close no deal that isn't genuinely good for the client.
- **Move off the solution**: when a client asks for a thing ("we need Power BI"), the professional response is to move *off* it and onto the problem — "no guessing."
- Structure the inquiry: **Issues → Evidence → Impact → Context → Constraints**. Turn soft statements into numbers: "How much? How do you know? What does it cost you?"
- **Qualify hard** (opportunity, resources, decision process); honor **yellow lights** — voice concerns early instead of hoping they resolve.

**Contribution to Customer Impact:**
- This is the *literal conversational method* for our biggest mechanical problem: leads arriving tech-labeled. "Move off the solution" is the trained behavior that converts "we want Fabric" into "orders ship 4 days late and it costs $2M a year."
- Evidence → Impact questioning *is* baseline-building in conversation form; the Impact Hypothesis is its written output. Our discovery training should be built on this book.
- "Let's get real or let's not play" gives sellers permission to disqualify: no executive owner of the metric, no real budget for the outcome → it's a tech project; price it as one or walk.

**They would ask us:**
1. Can every seller ask "what happens if you *don't* fix this?" and get to a dollar figure without a solution architect in the room? If not, what's the training plan?
2. What are your yellow lights for an outcome deal, and does your pipeline process force them into the open?
3. Are you prepared to disqualify revenue that doesn't fit the motion — publicly, in front of the sales team, in Q1?

## Seat 4 — Thomas Lah
### *Technology-as-a-Service Playbook* (with J.B. Wood) — The Economic Model Seat

**Core ideas:**
- **LAER** (Land–Adopt–Expand–Renew): in a subscription/consumption world, the money is made *after* the land — adoption drives expansion drives renewal.
- **"Swallow the fish"**: transitioning revenue models means costs rise before revenue recovers; leadership must plan the fish, not deny it.
- **Services engineering**: standardize, package, and price offers with known delivery variance — craft delivery can't scale and can't underwrite commitments.
- Profit horizons: manage the portfolio explicitly across margin today vs growth tomorrow. Outcome engineering: map tech capabilities to a *tight set* of target customer outcomes.

**Contribution to Customer Impact:**
- Supplies the **economic argument** for the whole plan: Microsoft's own incentives (MAU, ACR, adoption accelerators) have moved to LAER economics; a partner still monetizing only the "Land" (projects) is fighting the ecosystem's cash flow.
- VRRs and Impact Reviews are our Adopt/Expand/Renew machinery — they're not account management garnish, they're the revenue model.
- Warns us honestly: the 2027 pilot has fish-swallowing costs (unbillable Outcome Owner time, measurement infrastructure, comp accelerators). Budget them explicitly or the CFO kills the motion mid-transition.

**They would ask us:**
1. What does your fish look like — how much margin do you consciously give up in 2027, and where's the line item?
2. Which offers are *engineered* (fixed scope, known variance, repeatable) versus crafted? You can only attach outcome language to the engineered ones.
3. What % of revenue is renewable (managed impact, subscriptions) by end of 2027 — and who owns that number?

## Seat 5 — Matthew Dixon & Brent Adamson
### *The Challenger Sale* — The Insight Seat

**Core ideas:**
- Star performers in complex sales are **Challengers**, not relationship builders: they **teach** the customer something new about the customer's own business, **tailor** to stakeholders, and **take control** of the sale (comfort with money talk and constructive tension).
- **Commercial insight**: the teaching must lead uniquely back to your differentiation — "reframe how they see their business, then show why you're the best answer to the reframed problem."
- Solution selling died because customers now self-diagnose; suppliers who just respond to stated needs get commoditized and price-shopped.

**Contribution to Customer Impact:**
- Explains *why* tech-labeled leads are a trap: a customer who has already decided "we need Power BI" has done their own diagnosis — responding to it makes us a comparable commodity. The outcome reframe is a **Challenger teach**, and it needs prepared commercial insight, not just good questions.
- Our insight ammunition must be built, not improvised: per-play benchmark data ("distributors your size close in 12 days; best-in-class close in 5 — here's what the 7 days cost"), drawn from our delivery history and VRR records. The Outcome Catalog should evolve into a library of teaching narratives.
- Take control: outcome deals involve more stakeholders and longer cycles; sellers must drive process and talk money early — which Khalsa's method operationalizes.

**They would ask us:**
1. For each play, what is the *specific commercial insight* — the thing the customer doesn't know about their own business — and why does it lead uniquely to JourneyTeam?
2. Where will the benchmark data come from before you have VRR history? (Microsoft TEI studies, industry benchmarks, your own project archive?)
3. Are you hiring/coaching for Challenger behaviors, or assuming relationship builders will run this motion?

## Seat 6 — Thomas H. Davenport & Jeanne G. Harris
### *Competing on Analytics* — The Measurement Seat

**Core ideas:**
- Firms that **compete on analytics** make fact-based capability their distinctive edge: enterprise-wide data, committed leadership, targeted strategic use, analytical talent (the **DELTA** model: Data, Enterprise, Leadership, Targets, Analysts).
- Analytics maturity is a staged journey (analytically impaired → analytical competitor); ambition must match maturity.
- The highest-value analytics are embedded in decisions and processes, not in reports.

**Contribution to Customer Impact:**
- Two distinct contributions. **First, for our customers:** every Impact Hypothesis depends on the customer's ability to produce a metric. DELTA is our diagnostic — an analytically impaired customer can't do a gainshare deal, but "get you to measurable" is itself a sellable first outcome (and a Data & AI-led entry on almost every play).
- **Second, for us:** Customer Impact makes JourneyTeam an analytics competitor in its own operations — baselines, VRR telemetry, play benchmark libraries, pipeline leading indicators. The measurement backbone is a Data & AI practice deliverable to the *firm*.
- Justifies the plan's sequencing: measurement capability precedes outcome commitments (matches TSIA and the pricing research).

**They would ask us:**
1. What's your DELTA assessment of *JourneyTeam itself*? Do you have the data, leadership commitment, targets, and analysts to run a measurement-based business model?
2. For each play's anchor metric, where does the data physically live, and who refreshes it quarterly for the VRR?
3. Which target customers are analytically mature enough for outcome engagements — and is that in your qualification criteria?

## Seat 7 — Stephen Few
### *Information Dashboard Design* — The Communication Seat

**Core ideas:**
- A dashboard is a **single-screen, at-a-glance** display of the information needed to achieve one or more objectives — not a decoration.
- Design for the human: reduce non-data pixels, use visual attributes (position, length, color) according to perception science, no chartjunk, no meaningless variety.
- Most dashboards fail by prioritizing flash over meaning; clarity is a discipline.

**Contribution to Customer Impact:**
- The entire model culminates in an executive looking at a page and understanding *whether the promised impact happened*. If the VRR scorecard is cluttered, gamed, or vague, the credibility of the whole motion dies at that moment.
- Standardize a **one-page Impact Scorecard** per play: baseline → target → actual, adoption trend, dollars attributed, next opportunity. Same layout every quarter, every customer — comparability builds trust and makes case studies nearly free to produce.
- Also disciplines our internal metrics (doc 08): a monthly one-screen view leadership actually reads beats a 40-tile dashboard nobody does.

**They would ask us:**
1. Can a CFO absorb the Impact Scorecard in 30 seconds without explanation? Have you tested it on one?
2. Does every number on it answer a question someone actually has — and is the baseline always visible next to the actual?
3. Are you displaying measures of impact, or measures of activity dressed up as impact?

## Seat 8 — Richard P. Rumelt
### *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* — The Strategy Rigor Seat

**Core ideas:**
- Good strategy is a **kernel**: (1) a **diagnosis** naming the critical challenge, (2) a **guiding policy** for dealing with it, (3) **coherent actions** that reinforce each other.
- **Bad strategy** signatures: fluff (buzzwords masquerading as insight), failure to face the problem, mistaking goals for strategy ("25% multi-practice revenue!" is a goal, not a strategy), bad strategic objectives (a dog's dinner of the unconnected).
- Strategy is choice under scarcity — leverage comes from focusing strength on a pivotal point.

**Contribution to Customer Impact:**
- The board's chief skeptic. "Customer Impact" is exactly the kind of phrase that becomes fluff — Rumelt forces the kernel: **Diagnosis:** the merger changed structure but not incentives, artifacts, or motions, so single-practice behavior reproduced itself. **Guiding policy:** change the deal flow (outcome-first artifacts, attribution economics, adoption-era revenue) rather than the org chart. **Coherent actions:** plays + paid discovery + Impact Hypothesis gate + double-crediting + VRRs — each reinforcing the others.
- Tests coherence: if we launch outcome plays but keep pure single-practice quotas, the actions contradict the policy — that's bad strategy, predictably.
- Demands focus: two plays and a handful of accounts in the pilot is leverage; six plays across all accounts in Q1 is a dog's dinner.

**They would ask us:**
1. State your diagnosis in three sentences without the word "collaboration." Does everyone in leadership agree it's *the* problem?
2. Which of your actions actively contradict the guiding policy? (Look at comp, quotas, and marketing first.)
3. What are you choosing **not** to do in 2027 to fund this? If the answer is "nothing," it isn't a strategy.

## Seat 9 — A.G. Lafley & Roger L. Martin
### *Playing to Win* — The Strategic Choice Seat

**Core ideas:**
- Strategy is five linked choices — the **strategy cascade**: (1) What is our **winning aspiration**? (2) **Where to play**? (3) **How to win** there? (4) What **capabilities** must be in place? (5) What **management systems** are required?
- Playing to *play* (participating everywhere) is the most common losing strategy; you must make real where-to-play exclusions.
- Choices must nest: how-to-win must be true *for the chosen where-to-play*, and capabilities/systems must actually support it.

**Contribution to Customer Impact:**
- Converts the plan from a framework into **choices**. Where to play: which industries (2–3 per play, per the competitive research), which account tiers (existing multi-practice-potential accounts first), which buyer (the executive who owns the metric), which deal sizes (>$100K threshold). How to win: measured-impact proof + cross-practice breadth that single-stack competitors can't match + published pricing/credit-back transparency the mid-market doesn't offer.
- Forces capability honesty: value-quantification skill, Challenger-grade insight content, measurement infrastructure, Outcome Owner bench — capability gaps are plan workstreams, not assumptions.
- Management systems = docs 07/08 (attribution, deal desk, capacity view, metric cadence). Lafley/Martin insist these are *part of the strategy*, not implementation detail — which is precisely the lesson of the failed merger.

**They would ask us:**
1. Complete the sentence with real exclusions: "We will play in ___ and explicitly not in ___."
2. For each play: why do we win against a cheaper single-practice specialist *and* against a bigger firm like Avanade? If there's no answer, don't launch that play.
3. Which capability on your list doesn't actually exist today, and what's the build/buy/partner decision for it?

## Seat 10 — John Doerr
### *Measure What Matters* — The Execution Cadence Seat

**Core ideas:**
- **OKRs**: significant, action-oriented Objectives with measurable Key Results — the "what" and the "how much," set quarterly/annually, **transparent to everyone**.
- Superpowers: focus & commit to priorities; align & connect work; track for accountability; **stretch** for amazing. Separate committed OKRs from aspirational ones.
- **CFRs** (Conversations, Feedback, Recognition) carry the culture change that OKRs alone can't; decouple OKR scores from compensation to keep goals honest and ambitious.

**Contribution to Customer Impact:**
- The transformation needs an execution operating system or it dissolves into business-as-usual by March. OKRs give the shift a visible, shared, quarterly heartbeat — exactly the "leader-owned narrative + inspected metrics" the reorg research says separates the 23% of successful transformations.
- Structure: one company Objective ("Prove customers buy outcomes from JourneyTeam") with committed KRs from doc 08's leading indicators (discoveries sold, hypotheses attached, pilot conversions, VRRs held); each practice sets *aligned but self-authored* OKRs — Doerr is explicit that bottom-up OKRs create ownership, which is how four practice directors become co-owners instead of conscripts.
- A crucial nuance vs doc 07: comp accelerators pay on *outcomes of deals*; OKR scores stay **decoupled from comp** so teams set honest stretch targets. Both, not either.

**They would ask us:**
1. What is the *one* company-level Objective for the pilot quarter, and can every seller recite it?
2. Which KRs are committed (sandbagging not allowed) vs aspirational (failure expected and safe)?
3. Where's the weekly/monthly CFR cadence — who has the conversation when a practice's KRs go red, and is it a coaching conversation or a blame one?

## How we'll use the board

1. **Design reviews:** each major doc (framework, plays, sales motion, operating model, roadmap) gets a "board pass" — we answer the 30 questions above in writing where they bite.
2. **Board memos:** for contested decisions (pricing model, comp design, pilot scope), write the strongest argument each relevant seat would make, then decide. Disagreement between seats (e.g., Maister's trust caution vs Dixon/Adamson's constructive tension; Doerr's decoupling vs comp accelerators) is where the real design work lives — surface it, don't average it.
3. **Session log:** board sessions get captured as numbered docs in this folder (`01-session-*.md`).

## Known tensions on the board (worth exploiting)

| Tension | Seats | Design question it forces |
|---|---|---|
| Trust vs. tension | Maister/Green/Galford ↔ Dixon/Adamson | How do we challenge customers' thinking without raising perceived self-orientation? |
| Client responsibility vs. taking control | Block ↔ Dixon/Adamson | Who drives the engagement — and when does "taking control of the sale" violate 50/50? |
| Stretch goals vs. contracted outcomes | Doerr ↔ Lah/Khalsa | Internal targets can stretch; customer commitments must be underwritten. Keep the two vocabularies separate. |
| Strategy as exclusion vs. four practices' growth plans | Rumelt, Lafley/Martin ↔ practice P&Ls | Every practice wants its tech in every play. Who decides what's out? |
